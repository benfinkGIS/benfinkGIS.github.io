What Would Street Trees Do for a Bare Tucson Neighborhood?

Since 1996 the residents of Dunbar Spring, just north of downtown Tucson, have planted their own street trees: by their count more than 1,700 native trees, set in basins that catch rain off the street through cuts in the curb. Tree canopy now covers … of the neighborhood.

Western Hills, about four miles to the south-east, is also mostly houses on small lots. Its canopy covers …. This page maps both neighborhoods from aerial lidar and imagery, works out where a tree could go in each, and plants the same four native species Dunbar Spring uses to see what would change: canopy, afternoon shade, carbon and surface heat.

    What each planting would change in …

    Street trees go only in the public strip beside the street, as Dunbar Spring’s did. Streets and homes adds up to two trees on each home lot and the edges of parks. Full build-out fills every spot a tree would fit and is an upper bound, not a proposal. Each one includes the one before.

    How far to trust the map underneath

    The land cover was checked against … points chosen at random and labelled by hand from the 2023 aerial photo, without sight of the map. Tree canopy holds up: … of what the map calls canopy is canopy. What the ground is made of is harder. Only … of mapped pavement is pavement; packed dirt lots and shaded yards read as asphalt. That error hides plantable ground and does not invent it, so the plantings above err on the small side.

    The map was revised once after the first scoring, which showed shade being mapped as low plants, and was scored again against the same labels. Canopy was not changed.     Data sources and methods

    • What this is: a planning sketch. It shows where trees would fit and roughly what they would do. It is not a planting plan: nothing here checks buried utilities, who owns a strip of ground, or who would water a tree through its first summers.
    • Lidar: the U.S. Geological Survey’s 3D Elevation Program, Pima County, flown 27 September to 17 November 2021, about … points per square metre here. Mesquite was still in leaf. Canopy is anything 2.5 m or taller that is neither ground nor building and is green in the photo or lets the laser through. Roofs come from the lidar’s own building class.
    • Imagery: National Agriculture Imagery Program, four bands at 0.3 m, flown 18 October 2023. A tree planted or lost between the two flights counts as a map error.
    • Land cover: lidar decides trees and roofs. A Random Trees classifier in ArcGIS Pro sorts the rest into pavement, low plants and bare ground from the imagery and the strength of the laser’s return, trained on samples drawn by rule (the middle of a street is pavement) so that no training area is drawn by hand. Low plants and bare ground could not be told apart reliably and are shown together as open ground.
    • Accuracy check: 25 points in each mapped class in each neighborhood. … the labeller could not call were left out. Figures are weighted by each class’s share of the map. The labels were made quickly, a second or two each.
    • Where a tree can go: open ground at least 3 m from a roof, 1 m from pavement, 2 m from an existing crown, 3 m from overhead lines found in the lidar, and 10 m from a street corner. Street trees stay within 5 m of the pavement. Yards are home lots only; parks and schools take trees on their outer 10 m; shops, industry and railroad land are left out. These distances are assumptions for a sketch, not a city or utility standard.
    • Which tree goes where: a species has to fit clear of roofs and wires. Among those that fit, the planting is steered toward a mix of 30% velvet mesquite, 25% each palo verde and 20% desert willow, and desert willow, the thirstiest of the four, goes only where runoff collects. The mix and that rule are choices made here.
    • Runoff: water is routed downhill over the lidar ground surface, with roofs shedding outward. Tucson’s normal rainfall is 10.61 inches a year (National Weather Service, 1991 to 2020), so … m² of roof and pavement sheds about 3,500 gallons a year before losses.
    • Shade: shadows cast from tree crowns and roofs at 4 p.m. on 15 July, when the sun is …° up and due west. Trees are treated as solid, so this is where shade falls, not how deep it is; palo verde shade is thin. “Street edge” is the 2 m beside the pavement on both sides, … km of it in …. Neither neighborhood has a mapped sidewalk layer. The same method reproduces the shadows in the 2023 photo: … of bare-ground cells agree in ….
    • Carbon: the canopy each planting adds, times … kg of carbon per square metre of urban tree cover, the U.S. average in Nowak and others (2013). The range uses the lowest and highest city in that study (… and …). None of its cities is in the Sonoran Desert, and open-crowned desert trees probably sit low. It counts the trees alone, once mature.
    • Surface temperature: two satellites, at two times of day. Both read the temperature of roofs, pavement and soil, not of the air. Late morning is Landsat 8 and 9, the median of June to August scenes for 2021 to 2023, through Google Earth Engine, at about 11 a.m. Afternoon is ECOSTRESS, NASA’s sensor on the space station, which passes at varying hours: of … scenes taken between 1 and 6 p.m. in June to August of 2021 to 2025, … were clear enough to use, typically at about … Each was lined up against the Landsat map (the largest move was … m) and they were combined as differences from the citywide median. The change comes from how temperature varies with tree canopy across … built-up blocks of … m in Tucson, allowing for pavement and elevation. Canopy across the city is from Meta and the World Resources Institute’s canopy height map, which tracks the lidar canopy closely in the two neighborhoods (correlation …). Comparing each block only with others within about … km, the surface is … °C cooler for every ten points of canopy in late morning and … °C in the afternoon; comparing across the whole city gives … and … °C. The table shows the first of each pair, with the second beneath it. The afternoon sensor is blurrier than Landsat, which accounts for part of its smaller figure but not all of it. This is how leafy and bare blocks differ today, not an experiment: leafy blocks also tend to be watered, which desert trees living on runoff would not be, so the real effect may be smaller. A fit from the two neighborhoods alone gave … °C.
    • Linked canopy: tree crowns, including those just outside the boundary, are grouped into clusters with gaps of … m or less. “Linked to riparian habitat” means the cluster reaches ground on Pima County’s riparian classification map. Washes and parks are from Pima County GIS.
    • Species records: the Global Biodiversity Information Facility search service, queried …, which carries iNaturalist’s research-grade observations and eBird’s observation dataset among others. Records since … with coordinates and no flagged location problem. This is a search, not a citable download.
    • Boundaries: City of Tucson neighborhood associations; the city registers Western Hills as “Western Hills II”. Both neighborhoods were picked from a screen of all 154 by how green they are in the 2023 imagery. Other layers: Pima County GIS (parcels, streets, parks). Basemap: Esri.
    • 3D views: ground and buildings are the lidar surface with the 2023 photo draped over it; walls are tinted plain. Today’s trees are the lidar canopy surface itself. Each proposed tree is a simple crown at its published mature height and width. The viewer draws them live in the browser, with the sun placed by the same equations as the shade figures; the stills are rendered in Blender.
    • Scripts: tools/canopy/ in the site’s repository, run in order; the analysis runs in ArcGIS Pro’s Python. tucson-canopy/README.md has the full method and every figure.
