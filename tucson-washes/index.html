Every Wash Between the Tucson Mountains and the Catalinas

Northwest Tucson sits between two mountain ranges, and the dry washes that cross it are the closest thing the city has to a habitat network: strips of mesquite, palo verde and open sand that run from the foothills to the Santa Cruz, the Rillito and the Cañada del Oro. Pima County maps … km of them in this area. The lidar shows a good deal more.

This page traces every channel that drains at least … hectares, from a one-metre elevation model, across … km² from Grant Road to Tangerine Road. Each reach carries the county’s name for it where there is one, its riparian class, the tree canopy along it, and the bobcat, vermilion flycatcher and queen butterfly records beside it. Every place a wash meets a road is marked as a bridge, a culvert or a dip, and a movement model shows where each of the three animals could get through. The model was then run again with one repair at a time, to rank the gaps where a rebuilt crossing or a planted strip would add the most. A sample of all of it was then checked by eye against aerial photos, and the results of that check are set out below, including where the method fell short.

    What the lidar adds

    The elevation model traces … km of line where water would run. Not all of that is wash. … km runs down streets: in much of Tucson the street is the storm drain, and in older neighborhoods the street is where a wash used to be. Another … km crosses ground with no channel cut into it: a yard, a parking lot, a sheet of open desert. Both are left off the map unless you turn them on.

    That leaves … km of wash: channel cut at least … cm into the ground, or mapped by the county. Of it, … km (…) is more than … m from any wash on the county’s layer: side channels, headwater fingers in the foothills, and neighborhood washes between back walls. Switch the map to “Who has mapped it” to see them. Going the other way, the lidar channels land on … of the county’s network.

    Checked by eye

    … spots were drawn at random and labelled by hand from 30 cm aerial photos, without sight of what the method had said: 160 channel reaches and 180 road crossings. Figures in brackets are 95% ranges.

    • Is it a wash? Of everything first traced as a channel, … was a wash to the eye. The rest was yard, lot or road edge. That finding is where the … cm rule above comes from. Of the reaches the rule keeps, … are washes, and it keeps … of the real ones. It is far from clean in the other direction: only … of what it drops is truly not a wash, so some small real washes are among the flow paths.
    • Is it a crossing? Of the points mapped where a wash meets a road, … are real crossings. The rest are places where the wash stops short, runs alongside, or the county’s street line is a driveway. Points typed open span are reliable: … are real. Culverts, at …, and at-grade dips, at …, are much less so.
    • Is the type right? Where there is a real crossing, the type agrees with the photo … of the time.
    • What are the beds made of? Of reaches that are washes: natural bed and banks …, natural bed between built banks (soil cement, riprap, walls) …, concrete …. These are shares from the sample, not a map. An attempt to map concrete from the colour of the imagery failed: the washes off the Catalinas run on pale granite sand that looks the same from above, and only … reaches it flagged were concrete.

    Road crossings

    … crossings are mapped: … open spans, … culverts and … at-grade dips. The type comes from the shape of the ground. A bridge deck is not part of bare-ground lidar, so a road that drops away at a wash and picks up on the far side is an open span. A road that stays level while the ground beside it falls into a channel is sitting on fill, and the wash is in a pipe. A road that follows the ground down and up again goes through the wash bed.

    For an animal following a wash these are three different things: an open span is a way under, a culvert is a pipe that may or may not be big enough, and a dip means crossing the pavement. Given the check above, read the culvert and at-grade points as likely, not certain.

    Where animals could move

    A wash on a map is not yet a way through the city. To see which ones could work as routes, the area was cut into 10 m squares and each square given a cost to cross, separately for three animals that get about in different ways. A circuit model, Omniscape, then treats the ground like a sheet of resistors: movement flows from every piece of habitat to all the habitat within a set distance, and the model adds up how much passes through each square. Pick an animal under “Modelled movement” in the settings to see the result. The map sets each square’s total against what plain open ground would carry, so coloured ground is where many possible routes squeeze into a strip. It shows where movement would concentrate if the costs are right; it is not a record of where animals are.

    • Bobcat, on foot, reaching … km. Washes, mapped riparian habitat and open desert are easy going, and ground gets harder the more of it is built on. The road costs follow the Bobcats in Tucson collar study, which tracked 38 cats on the west side of town. They crossed minor streets about as often as chance, crossed major roads about a quarter less, did not seek out culverts, and only one ever got across Interstate 10, through an underpass. So here minor streets cost nothing extra, major roads a little, and the interstate is a wall except where a river passes under it. About … of the area comes out as ground that gathers bobcat movement into a strip, and … of that ground lies within … m of a wash, where … of the whole area does. The Rillito, the Cañada del Oro and the Santa Cruz carry the strongest strips. Where the first two pass under the interstate, their bridges carry about … times the modelled movement of an average stretch of it.
    • Vermilion flycatcher, flying, reaching … km. Its habitat is lawn with trees, better near water: parks, golf courses and ball fields. Bare desert costs more to cross and solid development more again. Its map is a scatter of islands around golf courses, parks and the ponds along the Santa Cruz, joined where tree-lined strips run between them. Washes matter to it only where they carry trees or water: … of wash squares gather its movement.
    • Queen butterfly, reaching … km. Any green ground is easy, and washes and riparian strips easiest: its caterpillars feed on the climbing milkweeds that grow along them. Pavement and rooftops are the hard part. Washes were given the lowest cost, so this map follows them almost by construction: … of wash squares gather its movement. Read it as a picture of that assumption.

    “All three” puts the maps together. … of the area gathers the movement of at least two of the animals and … of all three, and … of that shared ground lies within … m of a wash: the Rillito, the Cañada del Oro, the Santa Cruz and the foothill washes that feed them.

    Checked against sightings. People photograph animals where people are, so each animal’s iNaturalist records were compared with the records of everything else in its group (other mammals, other birds, other butterflies) and one question asked: where someone was out recording, did this animal turn up on ground with more modelled movement (the model’s total for the square, before it is set against open ground)? Take one record of the animal and one of something else at random; the share of the time the animal’s record sits on the higher value is the score, and 50% is a coin toss. The flycatcher scores …: its records do sit on ground with more modelled movement than other birds’ records, and they are … times as common as expected in the fifth of the area with the most. The bobcat scores … and the queen …, no better than chance: their records turn up in much the same places as other mammals and other butterflies. For those two, the maps show what follows from the costs chosen, and the sightings neither support nor contradict them.

    Every cost in the model is a judgement, listed with its reasons in the project’s README. Movement fades within one reach of the edge of the map, because the model knows nothing of the ground beyond it. The flycatcher’s map stops … km from each patch of habitat because that is the reach it was given, not because the bird stops there. Culvert sizes are unknown, so the bobcat model was run twice more, with every mapped culvert shut and with every one as open as a bridge: the result changes by more than a quarter on … of wash squares.

    Where a repair would do the most

    The movement model can answer a what-if: repair one place, run it again, and see how much more movement that place carries. That was done for … candidate gaps. Each animal is stopped by different things, so each has its own kind of gap and its own kind of repair.

    • Bobcat: a road crossing rebuilt as an open span. Every place a wash meets the Interstate 10 corridor, taking the freeway, its ramps and frontage roads and the railroad beside it as one obstacle (… places); every crossing of Oracle Road that is not already a bridge (…); and the … most promising crossings of other major roads.
    • Vermilion flycatcher: trees planted along a wash. … stretches of 200 to 800 m, planted until … of the ground within … m of the channel is under canopy.
    • Queen butterfly: a break in a wash planted as a strip. … places where the traced channel leaves its wash and runs down a street or over open ground, for up to 800 m, before it is a wash again.

    The best five for each animal, no two closer than … km, so one long problem does not fill the list. “Movement” is the modelled movement through the gap as a multiple of what open ground would carry, as it is now and with the repair. “Added” is the movement the repair adds through the gap and the … m either side of it, with that animal’s best gap set to 100. Click a gap’s letter to see it on the map.

    Bobcat: the freeway. Interstate 10 is a wall in this model except where the Rillito and the Cañada del Oro pass under it, and … of the six best candidates sit within 3 km of B1, from Avra Valley Road to just past Twin Peaks Road, where open desert reaches the freeway from both sides. B1 and B2 stand for them. A culvert there carries about what open ground would; opened into a span it carries some twenty times that, because it becomes the only way through for kilometres. The place is no discovery. An April 2025 report describes the stretch between Twin Peaks Road and Avra Valley Road as one of the few places along the freeway where wildlife can cross, with an existing underpass and a county-funded ramp over the river levee then being built. The model knows about neither, and reaches the same stretch from land cover and road data alone; a site visit should start at that underpass. Away from the freeway, the best crossing of Oracle Road adds … as much as B1 and the best crossing of any other major road …. The collar study found those roads slow bobcats without stopping them, so the model gains little from rebuilding them.

    Vermilion flycatcher: washes between the greens. Its gaps are stretches of wash that run between golf courses, parks and ponds with few trees of their own: the Cañada del Oro between two sets of fairways, the Santa Cruz between Sweetwater Wetlands and Silverbell Golf Course. Planting them turns ground the bird has to cross into a line it can follow from perch to perch. This is the one list that rests on a model the sightings support.

    Queen butterfly: washes that became streets. Every listed gap is a place where a wash was built over and its water now runs down a street or across a car park. The gains are close together (the fifth adds … as much as the first), so the kind of repair matters more than the order: a planted strip along the street, of the sort Tucson’s green stormwater program already builds as curb-cut rain gardens.

    This is a shortlist for a site visit, not a design. What is there now was read from aerial photos; nobody has stood at any of these places, and cost, ownership and flood engineering are not weighed. Only the flycatcher list rests on a model the sightings support. The model moves the same total whatever is repaired, so a repair shows up as movement drawn to the gap from somewhere harder; a gap that is already the only way through gains little by this measure even where opening it would help. Each repair changes how easily an animal gets across and leaves the amount of habitat as it is, so planting that would become habitat itself is undercounted. Gaps near the edge of the map are understated.

    The named washes

    The … washes the county names in this area. Length is the county’s own centerline; the other figures are measured along the lidar channels that carry the name. Click a name to find it on the map, or a heading to sort.

    The record counts say where people have photographed these animals, not where the animals are. A wash with a path beside it will always out-score one behind a row of houses. “Drains” counts ground inside the study area only, so the Santa Cruz, the Rillito and the Cañada del Oro, which arrive already carrying water from far upstream, are larger than their figures. Canopy is measured within 10 m of the channel line, so for those three wide rivers it describes what grows in the bed, not the trees on the banks.

    Reading the riparian classes

    Pima County’s riparian habitat map, adopted in 2005 and edited since, grades the vegetation along washes. … of the traced wash length falls inside it.

    • Class H, hydroriparian and mesoriparian: water at or near the surface for much of the year. Cottonwood and willow country.
    • Class A to D, xeroriparian: the usual dry wash, graded by how much vegetation it carries. A is the densest, D the sparsest.
    • Not mapped: either too sparse to qualify, or never surveyed. Most of the small channels the lidar adds are here.

    Data sources and methods

    • Elevation: U.S. Geological Survey 3DEP one-metre bare-ground model from the Pima County lidar flight of autumn 2021, twelve tiles.
    • Channels: ArcGIS Pro Spatial Analyst. Pits are filled, each cell drains to its lowest neighbour, and a channel begins where … hectares drain to one cell. Channels are split into reaches at each confluence.
    • Wash or flow path: a reach is a wash when its line sits at least … cm below the average ground within 7 m, or at least half of it is on the county’s wash layer or riparian map; and under a fifth of it is on street pavement. The depth cut-off was chosen from the hand labels, and the scores above are cross-validated: each labelled reach was typed by a cut-off chosen without it.
    • Culverts: bare-ground lidar keeps road and rail embankments but not the pipes under them. Where a county-mapped wash crosses a street or the railroad, a strip 20 m wide is lowered to channel-bed level so the water passes. Unmapped washes pond behind their roads in the model and spill over the low point of the crown, so their line can be off by some metres at a crossing.
    • Storm water in streets: a reach with more than half its length on street pavement (the county centerline widened by 6 to 12 m a side, by road class).
    • Names and the county network: Pima County Regional Flood Control District’s Wash Network. A reach takes a name when at least half of it lies within … m of a county wash with that name; the distance is 30 m for washes the county rates at 2,000 cubic feet per second or more and 60 m for those over 10,000, whose beds are wider than the lidar’s single thread. Where two named washes meet, the larger keeps its name. Share of the county network found by the lidar, by the county’s flow class: ….
    • Riparian classes: Pima County’s regulatory riparian classification (2005). A reach takes the class that covers most of it, if at least 30% of the reach is mapped. Important Riparian Areas are shown by their underlying class.
    • Tree canopy: National Agriculture Imagery Program aerial photos (…), four bands, resampled to 1 m. A cell is canopy with an …. Those cut-offs are the ones that agree best with the lidar canopy map of two Tucson neighborhoods from a separate project, where a person checked it point by point. Against that map the imagery rule agrees on … of cells in the two neighborhoods, finds … of the lidar canopy, and … of what it calls canopy is. Tall shrubs and reeds can pass for trees, and shaded crowns can be missed. The photos were flown on three dates, so the rule may read slightly greener on one flight line than the next.
    • Wildlife records: research-grade iNaturalist observations with an open position stated to 100 m or better: …. Each is counted at the nearest wash reach within … m. Individual points are not shown, since many were taken in people’s yards.
    • Crossings: where a wash reach meets a county street centerline or the railroad; the two sides of a divided road count once, and points where the wash runs within 25° of the road are left out. Each is measured on the lidar before the culvert cuts. Open span: the road centerline changes level by … m or more within 6 m. Culvert: no such step, and the ground along the channel stands … m or more above a line fitted through the bed either side. At-grade: neither. Both cut-offs were chosen from the hand labels and scored the same cross-validated way. Crossings of the interstate and the railroad that show neither are typed culvert by assumption. Pima County’s flood-map structures and the National Bridge Inventory are shown where they name the structure, and were not used to set the type.
    • The hand check: 60 crossings of each first-pass type and 160 reaches, drawn at random within groups and weighted back to the whole when scored. Labelled over two sessions from the 2023 photos, at a median of two and a half seconds each, by one person; nothing was checked on the ground.
    • Movement model: Omniscape 0.6.2 on a 10 m grid. Ground layers: percent impervious from the National Land Cover Database (2021, 30 m); canopy, other green ground and turf from the 2023 photos; roads by class from county centerlines; the washes, riparian classes and crossings above; ponds from the National Hydrography Dataset. National Bridge Inventory bridges over water are treated as open spans across the full width of the road. Each animal has a resistance surface and a habitat surface built from those layers; the numbers are in tucson-washes/resistance.json.
    • Sighting check: research-grade iNaturalist records placed to 100 m or better, thinned to one per 100 m square (… bobcat, … flycatcher and … queen squares against …, … and … for the rest of each group). Modelled movement is averaged over 50 m before it is read. Ranges are 95%, from resampling 1 km squares.
    • Gap ranking: each candidate changes the animal’s resistance surface in a few cells (a crossing to the cost of an open span; a planted stretch to the cost of ground with full perch cover; a broken stretch to the cost of a wash) and leaves the habitat surface alone. Omniscape is rerun, with and without the change, on a cut-out reaching two window radii beyond it and only for the windows the change can reach; a test against running every window gave the same answer to nine decimal places. Added movement is the change in current over the repair and the … m either side, divided by its length along the wash. Bobcat reruns step the window 510 m at a time instead of 250 m to save time; … of the leading bobcat gaps were repeated at 250 m, which left their order unchanged and moved the added movement by at most ….
    • Not included: channel width, the size of each culvert, and bed material reach by reach. Flow in the model is one-directional, so braided channels on the bajada are drawn as a single thread.
    • Basemap: Esri. Scripts: tools/washes/ in the site’s repository.
