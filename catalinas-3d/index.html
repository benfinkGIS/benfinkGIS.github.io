The Santa Catalinas in 3D

WORK IN PROGRESS. The model and the viewer are a first version.

The Santa Catalina Mountains stand over the north side of Tucson. Inside this model the ground climbs about 6,950 feet (2,120 m), from the desert floor to the top of Mount Lemmon. It is a model of the whole range at true scale, built from USGS elevation data with aerial photos laid over it.

The photos’ own sunlight and shadows were removed first, so the light you see is worked out from the shape of the ground. Move the sun and the shadows follow.

Data sources and methods

  • Area: a rectangle 46 km wide and 42 km tall (about 29 by 26 miles), from Oro Valley east to the San Pedro side and from the Rillito north to Oracle.
  • Elevation: USGS 3D Elevation Program, read at 10 m cells for the Blender renders. The viewer uses a lighter copy at 22.5 m cells so it loads quickly. Heights are true scale unless you switch the Height setting.
  • Imagery: USDA National Agriculture Imagery Program aerial photos, served by the USGS, at 4 m per pixel for the renders. The viewer starts at about 11 m per pixel; Sharper Imagery loads about 6 m.
  • Removing the photos’ sunlight: the photos were flown at different times of day, so each strip has its own shadows. A fit of photo brightness against the direction each slope faces, made separately around every spot, is divided out. Slopes in full shadow are recoloured from the sunlit ground next to them. Slopes that are darker because more grows on them, mostly north-facing ones, get lightened too, and the join between two flight strips is still faintly visible.
  • Light in the viewer: the sun follows its real path over Tucson on a day near the equinox (late March or late September). Times are sun time, where noon is when the sun is due south; clock time in Tucson runs about half an hour later. Shadows are found by stepping across the terrain toward the sun. Sky colour and brightness are set by eye.
  • Light in the renders: Blender’s physical sky model and Cycles renderer. The haze and the flat ground beyond the model in the first image are there for looks.
  • Summits: names and positions from the USGS Geographic Names Information System. Heights shown are read from the elevation data at those points and can differ from surveyed heights by a few feet to a few tens of feet.

Project checklist

  • Elevation and imagery for the whole range
  • Photo sunlight removed from the imagery
  • Golden-hour renders in Blender
  • Interactive viewer with a movable sun
  • Close-up views from 1 m lidar
  • Roads, trails and canyon names
